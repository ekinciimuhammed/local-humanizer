# Blind editorial review

Reviewed only `blind-review-input.json`. Letters are treated as anonymous choices. This review assesses meaning, editorial improvement, and preservation of voice; it makes no assumptions about authorship or detector performance. Leading blank lines do not affect the editorial rankings. The research item has no C candidate, so a complete four-way aggregate ranking is not possible.

## Generic AI overview

**Editorial ranking: C > A > D > B. Fidelity ranking: D > A ≈ C > B. None substantially improves the generic overview.**

- **C** offers the best modest improvement in readability. “A key benefit is improved efficiency” avoids repeating the subject, and “Organizations should carefully weigh the opportunities and potential risks of adopting AI” is shorter without losing the recommendation. All four named concerns remain. “AI can create significant value when implemented responsibly” retains possibility rather than promising a result. The move from how people and organizations “operate” to how they “work” slightly narrows that sentence toward employment, although daily life remains explicit in the opening. “This frees employees” retains the original's asserted consequence of faster completion. The stock benefit/concern/responsibility structure and abstract conclusion remain largely intact.
- **A** improves a few stiff connections: “That lets employees” and “carefully weigh” read easily. “Built into” is a reasonable colloquial rendering of “integral,” though it emphasizes incorporation rather than importance. It preserves faster completion, the concerns, the recommendation, and the explicit uncertainty of “has the potential.” Repeated full uses of “artificial intelligence” and the unchanged final paragraph leave the passage predominantly as it was. This is light editing, not a substantial change in naturalness.
- **D** is the closest semantic match. Reordering “Improved efficiency is a key benefit” and joining two sentences do little to improve the passage as a whole. The claims and their strength remain intact. Its high fidelity should not be mistaken for a meaningful transformation.
- **B** changes more wording but introduces avoidable drift. “Intelligent systems can now handle work that used to require significant manual effort and time” no longer explicitly says the work finishes faster; that benefit is merely implied by the efficiency framing. More seriously, “Organizations can minimize negative effects” strengthens the original “reducing potential negative effects”: minimizing suggests a greater degree of mitigation, and dropping “potential” treats the negative effects less cautiously. “Balancing” innovation with ethics also replaces the original instruction to combine them. “Staple,” “primary advantage,” and “boost in efficiency” are largely synonym substitutions. The passage remains generic despite greater lexical movement.

All candidates retain the distinction between a recommendation that organizations **should** consider risks and the claim that responsible implementation **can** offer value. B's concern is stronger mitigation language, not a conversion of the whole passage into an obligation or a guarantee.

## Cautious research summary

**Ranking: D > A > B. C was not supplied.**

- **D** makes a useful, restrained edit: it removes “It is worth noting,” “Furthermore,” “Moreover,” and “In conclusion,” which add little to the argument. This improves the reading experience more than the small edits in the generic overview, because there is identifiable verbal clutter to remove. “Employees reporting a quiet workspace also reported fewer interruptions” preserves the fact that both variables are self-reported. The fictional status, attribution, sample size, offices, percentages, sample boundary, voluntary participation, possible selection bias, unmeasured productivity, noncausal interpretation, exact quotation, and limits of follow-up research all survive.
- **A** also preserves the substantive constraints, including “employees who reported having a quiet workspace also reported fewer interruptions.” Combining the percentages works well, and “In that sample” keeps scope clear. Contractions are compatible with an accessible research summary. However, “For work arrangements” contributes little, and “All told” replaces one stock closing transition with another. The improvement is real but somewhat more interventionist than necessary; D is cleaner.
- **B** commits the clearest fidelity error in this set: “Those who **had** a quiet workspace also reported fewer interruptions” changes reported workspace conditions into asserted conditions. The original established what employees reported, not an independently verified workspace characteristic. Retaining the noncausal caveat does not repair that change in evidential status. Other major protections remain, including possible selection bias, sample-limited preferences, the exact quotation, unmeasured productivity, and the limits of a follow-up survey. “As for preferences” and “Ultimately” also retain formulaic transitions. Any fluency gain does not justify the evidence shift.

No supplied research candidate changes “could help determine” into an obligation to conduct a follow-up, or removes the warning that a follow-up alone would not establish causation. All preserve the quoted “should not guide staffing decisions” verbatim.

## Already natural control

**Ranking: C ≈ B ≈ D > A.** C preserves the original verbatim; B and D make the same single contraction, apart from D's leading whitespace. These three are editorially equivalent for practical purposes.

- **C** correctly leaves an already effective voice alone. Its rhythm, polite request, precise editing instructions, figurative brochure criticism, and relaxed timing all remain intact.
- **B / D** change only “you have” to “you've.” That is an acceptable optional copy edit, not evidence of a meaningful improvement. The voice and requested actions are preserved.
- **A** preserves the broad request and the distinctive “brochure asking for applause” image, but makes unnecessary changes. “The opening still **drags**” is firmer criticism than “still **feels slow**,” removing the explicit subjectivity and increasing the bluntness. “**Trim** the repeated explanation” can mean shorten it, whereas “**cut** the repeated explanation” more naturally requests its removal. These are small but real changes to tone and instruction. “Someone” and “once you've” are harmless alternatives, not clear improvements. No new fact, deadline, or corporate framing is added.

## Overall judgment

**D is the safest conservative editor across the three supplied items; A is generally faithful but sometimes edits for the sake of editing; B has the most consequential fidelity problems.** C is the preferred light edit of the generic overview and shows appropriate restraint on the control, but its research performance cannot be assessed.

The strongest successful intervention is removing empty transitions from the research summary while preserving the evidential qualifications. The control rewards restraint. The generic overview remains generic in every version: smoother local phrasing does not supply a more specific argument or distinctive voice, and adding unsupported examples or claims would not be an acceptable remedy in a fidelity-constrained edit.
